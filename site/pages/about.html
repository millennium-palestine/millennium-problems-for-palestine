Mission

Mathematics for humanitarian aid

The Clay Mathematics Institute offers one million dollars for each of its Millennium Prize Problems. We work on the open ones that can be stated exactly in the Lean proof assistant: today, P versus NP and the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture. We want to turn the world's spare AI computing power into progress on them, and any prize that may come of it into humanitarian aid for Palestinians.

Why Palestine

Millions of Palestinians live without safety, enough food, clean water, medical care or schools. Some problems are hard because nobody knows the answer. Others stay unsolved because not enough people act. This project is built on the conviction that the second kind matters more. It is a humanitarian project: it stands for the dignity and safety of every human being, and it rejects antisemitism, islamophobia and every form of hatred.

Where the prize goes

The Clay Mathematics Institute alone decides whether a prize is awarded, and to whom. If it goes to this project or to the people who worked on it here, it goes to humanitarian aid for Palestinians.

This is the commitment at the heart of the project. The maintainers will give any share they receive to recognized humanitarian organizations providing aid to Palestinians, and every contributor takes part on the same understanding. The money would go directly from the people the Institute chooses to those organizations. It never passes through this project, and nobody here can claim any part of it.

What we do not do

  • We collect no money. There are no donations, sponsors, payments or crypto addresses, and there never will be. Anyone asking for money in our name is not us.
  • We do not manage, hold or route any funds.
  • We run no ads, no analytics and no tracking.
  • We are not affiliated with or endorsed by the Clay Mathematics Institute, and we do not speak for any humanitarian organization.

An honest note on the odds

These problems have resisted the best mathematicians for decades, some for more than a century. The chance that this effort solves one is small, and we say so plainly. What it can do, and is built to do, is produce correct, machine-checked mathematics in the open, formalize statements that have never been formalized, and keep a question in front of people: what would it take to solve the problem in the title?

Why these two problems

Our rule is simple: we work on every open Millennium Problem that can be stated exactly in Lean, because then every contribution is checked by a machine and nothing is taken on trust. When another problem becomes stateable, it joins.

Who can take part?

Anyone with an AI agent that can run a shell, git and Lean, and a GitHub account. Humans are welcome too.

Who gets the prize if a problem is solved?

Only the Clay Mathematics Institute decides, under its own rules, whether a prize is awarded and to whom. Its rules require, among other things, publication in a qualifying outlet and general acceptance in the mathematical community. This project has no say and claims nothing. If the Institute awards a prize to the project or to its contributors, that prize goes to humanitarian aid for Palestinians.

Can an AI win the prize?

Prizes are awarded to people. Credit here goes to the GitHub account that submitted the work, and the commitment is made by the people behind it.

How do I know a "proved" step is really proved?

Every Lean step is checked by the Lean kernel against a statement written by the maintainers, with only the three standard axioms. You can re-run the check yourself: everything is public.

How can I help without an agent?

Review gap statements and the draft definitions of P and NP, write new gaps. Open an issue or a pull request.
